## FOUNDATIONS (1943–1969)

**1. McCulloch & Pitts, 1943, *Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics* — VERDICT: CORRECT.**
Warren S. McCulloch & Walter Pitts, "A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity," *Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics* **5**(4): 115–133, December 1943. DOI 10.1007/BF02478259. (The journal was renamed *Bulletin of Mathematical Biology* only in 1973, so the 1943 journal name is right.)
URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02478259

**2. Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," *Mind*, October 1950; originally the "imitation game" — VERDICT: CORRECT.**
*Mind* **59**(236): 433–460, October 1950. The paper opens with §1 "The Imitation Game"; "Can machines think?" is replaced by the imitation game. Turing never used the phrase "Turing test" — that label is later usage. **When the phrase "Turing test" was first coined: COULD NOT VERIFY** this session.
URL: https://academic.oup.com/mind/article/LIX/236/433/986238

**3. Dartmouth: summer 1956; 1955 proposal by McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, Shannon; term coined there — VERDICT: MOSTLY CORRECT, ONE FIX.**
The proposal is dated **31 August 1955** and signed J. McCarthy (Dartmouth), M. L. Minsky (Harvard), N. Rochester (IBM), C. E. Shannon (Bell Labs). Original wording: *"We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire."* **Fix:** the term "artificial intelligence" appears in the **1955 proposal**, not first at the 1956 workshop — say "named in the 1955 proposal; the workshop popularised it."
URL: https://www-formal.stanford.edu/jmc/history/dartmouth/dartmouth.html

**4. Rosenblatt: 1957 or 1958? Mark I hardware; 1958 *Psychological Review*; 1962 book — VERDICT: SPLIT.**
- Paper: **CORRECT.** "The perceptron: a probabilistic model for information storage and organization in the brain," *Psychological Review* **65**(6): 386–408, November 1958. DOI 10.1037/h0042519. https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2Fh0042519
- **1957** = the year Rosenblatt conceived the perceptron and simulated it on an **IBM 704** at Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory.
- **Mark I Perceptron hardware: the common "1957/1958" is WRONG.** The machine was assembled and tested at Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory between **June and 14 December 1959**; its **first public demonstration was 23 June 1960**. It had a 20×20 (400-cell) photocell retina, 512 association units, 8 response units, and random S→A wiring on a plugboard with weights in potentiometers driven by electric motors.
- Book: **CORRECT.** *Principles of Neurodynamics: Perceptrons and the Theory of Brain Mechanisms*, Spartan Books, 1962 (a published version of the 1961 report).
URL: https://handwiki.org/wiki/Perceptron

**5. Perceptron convergence theorem — VERDICT: CORRECT, but state the limits.**
It guarantees: if the training set is linearly separable with margin γ and all inputs satisfy ‖x‖ ≤ R, the perceptron learning rule makes **at most (R/γ)² mistakes** and then converges — finitely many updates, for any learning rate and any sampling order. The theorem is credited to Rosenblatt et al.; the short proof is due to **Novikoff (1962)**. Limits that must be stated: (a) it assumes linear separability — **no convergence guarantee for non-separable data** (the cycling theorem only bounds ‖w‖); (b) it says nothing about **generalisation**; (c) the solution reached is **arbitrary** — any separating hyperplane, not the best one (hence the later maximum-margin/SVM line of work); (d) the bound depends on the unknown margin γ.
URL: https://handwiki.org/wiki/Perceptron

**6. Minsky & Papert, *Perceptrons* (1969), XOR, and "it killed neural networks" — VERDICT: OVERSTATED.**
The book (MIT Press, 1969) proved that **single-layer ("order-1") perceptrons cannot compute XOR/parity**, and more generally that the connectedness predicate requires order Ω(n^1/2). **What it did NOT say:** Minsky and Papert already knew multilayer perceptrons *can* compute XOR; the book does not claim MLPs are limited. The real blocker was that **nobody in the 1960s had an algorithm for training hidden layers** (backpropagation came later). "It killed neural networks" is an overstatement — neural-net work continued, funding became hard to find, and the funding decline had multiple causes (notably the 1969 Mansfield Amendment redirecting DARPA to mission-oriented work). Rosenblatt died in a boating accident in 1971, not immediately after the book.
URLs: https://handwiki.org/wiki/Perceptron · https://handwiki.org/wiki/AI_winter

**7. Samuel's checkers, 1959, and the origin of "machine learning" — VERDICT: CORRECT.**
Arthur L. Samuel, "Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers," *IBM Journal of Research and Development* **3**(3): 210–229, July 1959. DOI 10.1147/rd.33.0210. It is the canonical origin of the term "machine learning" in print (it popularised rather than necessarily first-coined the phrase). The program itself predates the paper (work from 1952 onward). **Caution:** the popular claim that it beat Samuel himself is commonly repeated but is a claim about self-play results, not a tournament win — phrase it carefully.
URL: https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.33.0210

**8. The 1965 "one generation solves everything" claims; Simon's chess prediction; the 1970 *Life* quote — VERDICT: WRONG ON BOTH.**
- **Chess-in-10-years is 1957/1958, not 1965.** Simon's 1957 ORSA address, published as Simon & Newell, "Heuristic Problem Solving: The Next Advance in Operations Research," *Operations Research* **6**(1): 1–15, Jan–Feb 1958, listed: *"That within ten years a digital computer will be the world's chess champion, unless the rules bar it from competition"* (plus a new important theorem, music critics would accept, and most psychological theories as programs).
- **The "within twenty years… any work a man can do" line is 1960, not 1965.** It first appears in Simon, *The New Science of Management Decision* (Harper & Row, 1960), p. 38: *"Technologically, as I have argued earlier, machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work that a man can do."* It was **reprinted** in *The Shape of Automation for Men and Management* (1965), pp. 95–96 — which is why it is so often cited as 1965.
- **The 1970 *Life* quote is Minsky's, and it says 3–8 years, not 20.** Brad Darrach, "Meet Shaky, the First Electronic Person," *Life*, 20 November 1970: *"In from three to eight years we will have a machine with the general intelligence of an average human being."*
URLs: https://quoteinvestigator.com/2024/04/18/ai-prediction/ · https://quoteinvestigator.com/2020/11/11/ai-can-do/ · https://aiws.net/aiws-history-of-ai/the-history-of-ai/this-week-in-the-history-of-ai-at-aiws-net-marvin-minsky-was-quoted-in-life-magazine-in-from-three-to-eight-years-we-will-have-a-machine-with-the-general-intelligence-of-an-average-human-b/

**9. ALPAC 1966 and Lighthill 1973; cause or symptom? — VERDICT: MOSTLY CORRECT; Lighthill is BOTH.**
- **ALPAC:** committee established **April 1964**, chaired by **John R. Pierce**; report *Language and Machines: Computers in Translation and Linguistics*, National Academy of Sciences / National Research Council, Washington DC, **1966** (issued November 1966). It concluded machine translation was **more expensive, less accurate and slower than human translation**, and recommended funding computational linguistics, evaluation, and machine-*aided* translation instead. It did **not** say MT was impossible. US MT funding was cut dramatically afterwards; careers ended. Note the cuts were already beginning — ALPAC partly rationalised a decision already forming.
- **Lighthill:** *"Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey"*, commissioned by the UK **Science Research Council**, published **1973** in *Artificial Intelligence: A Paper Symposium* (Lighthill's own text is headed **July 1972**). Conclusion: *"In no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised"*; it identified combinatorial explosion as the core failure and was **supportive of computer-based CNS/neurophysiological modelling** while **highly critical of basic research in robotics and language processing**. It "formed the basis for the decision by the British government to end support for AI research in all but three universities" — **Edinburgh, Sussex and Essex**. It was contested in a 1973 BBC *Controversy* debate (Lighthill vs. Michie, McCarthy, Gregory).
- **Cause or symptom: both.** In the UK it was the proximate **cause** of the funding cuts. But it was itself a **symptom** of a wider loss of confidence, and in the US the operative causes were the **1969 Mansfield Amendment** and **1973–74 DARPA cutbacks**, not Lighthill.
URLs: https://www.chilton-computing.org.uk/inf/literature/reports/lighthill_report/p001.htm · https://ipfs.io/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Lighthill_Report.html · https://handwiki.org/wiki/ALPAC

## PARADIGM 1: SYMBOLIC AI AND EXPERT SYSTEMS (1969–1987)

**10. MYCIN — VERDICT: MOSTLY CORRECT; TWO FIXES.**
Developed from **1972** at Stanford's Heuristic Programming Project (CS Department + Divisions of Clinical Pharmacology and Infectious Disease) by Shortliffe, Buchanan, Davis and colleagues; the knowledge base was **"laid to rest in 1978"**. **Fix 1: the famous evaluation is 1979, not "the 1980s."** Yu, Fagan, Wraith, Clancey, Scott, Hannigan, Blum, Buchanan & Cohen, "Antimicrobial Selection by a Computer: A Blinded Evaluation by Infectious Diseases Experts," *JAMA* **242**(12): 1279–1282, **21 September 1979**: 8 infectious-disease evaluators × 10 meningitis cases — MYCIN's therapy was rated **acceptable in 65%** of cases (52/80) versus **42.5–62.5% (mean 55.5%)** for faculty specialists; on a majority-of-evaluators criterion MYCIN 70% vs faculty 44%; MYCIN never failed to cover a treatable pathogen. So "comparable to clinicians" is safe but **understates** it — MYCIN outscored every human prescriber in that small study. **Fix 2:** the metric is an **acceptability rating**, not "agreement %". There is a second, unblinded January 1979 study (Yu, Buchanan, Shortliffe et al., *Computer Programs in Biomedicine* 9(1): 95–102) where MYCIN met Stanford experts' standards **90.9%** of the time. **MYCIN was never used on patients** — the ward trial was intended but never undertaken.
URLs: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/480542/ · https://people.dbmi.columbia.edu/~ehs7001/Buchanan-Shortliffe-1984/Chapter-31.pdf · https://www.shortliffe.net/Buchanan-Shortliffe-1984/MYCIN%20Book.htm

**11. DENDRAL — VERDICT: CORRECT IN SUBSTANCE; START DATE IS 1964, NOT 1965.**
Lederberg's "Dendral-64" NASA interim report is dated **15 December 1964**; the project is conventionally dated 1965. Stanford University; **Edward Feigenbaum, Bruce G. Buchanan, Joshua Lederberg, Carl Djerassi** with a large team. Significance: generally called the **first expert system / first knowledge-based system**; introduced the **plan–generate–test** paradigm (later CONGEN), **knowledge engineering**, and **Meta-DENDRAL**, an early machine-learning rule-discovery system (a model for Mitchell's version-space work). By 1970 it performed structural interpretation at post-doc level. It directly inspired MYCIN, MOLGEN, PROSPECTOR, XCON. Name = "Dendritic Algorithm".
URL: https://handwiki.org/wiki/Dendral

**12. XCON/R1 at DEC — VERDICT: DEPLOYMENT CORRECT; RULE COUNT PARTLY WRONG; "$40M/YEAR" NOT SOURCED.**
Written in OPS5 by **John P. McDermott** at CMU (1978); **first went into use in 1980** at DEC's Salem, New Hampshire plant. Rule growth documented in Bachant & McDermott, "R1 Revisited: Four Years in the Trenches," *AI Magazine* **5**(3): 21, Fall 1984: **250** (Apr 1979) → **750** (Oct 1979) → **500** after the OPS5 rewrite (Apr 1980) → **850** (end 1980) → **~2,000** (Jul 1982) → **3,303** (Nov 1983). So "~2,500" is the right order for c.1982–83 but **understates the documented 3,303**; the "10,000 rules" figure is unverified for DEC-era XCON — do not use it. **The $40M/year figure is not primary-sourced:** the 1984 AI Magazine text contains **no dollar savings figure at all**. Documented figures are **~$25M/year** savings by the mid-1980s (after 80,000 orders at 95–98% accuracy) and Newquist's ***$40 million over just six years*** — i.e. the popular "$40M per year" appears to conflate "over six years" with "per year". Best practice: say "DEC estimated tens of millions of dollars a year in savings; the specific $40M/year figure is not traceable to a primary document."
URLs: https://ojs.aaai.org/aimagazine/index.php/aimagazine/article/view/445 · https://handwiki.org/wiki/Xcon · https://handwiki.org/wiki/AI_winter

**13. Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Systems project — VERDICT: DATES CORRECT; BUDGET NEEDS A FIX; SUCCESS IS CONTESTED.**
Launched **FY1982**, ran **11 years**, ended **FY1992 (March 1993)**, run by **ICOT** — and it **was extended by a two-year follow-on from FY1993**, so "ended 1992" needs that qualifier. **Budget — two corrections:** ICOT's own Final Evaluation Report says *"about ¥54 billion over 11 years"* (≈**$380M at 1990 exchange rates**). The commonly cited **¥57 billion is ~6% high**, and — more importantly — **the very widely repeated "$850 million" figure is NOT supported: it is roughly double the official number.** Do not use $850M. **Verdict on success: contested.** ICOT's final report claims substantial technical results (parallel inference machine prototypes, KL1/Prolog, knowledge-processing software) and international contribution; the widespread "abject failure" verdict rests on non-achievement of the original commercial/AI goals and the fact that the workstation/internet revolution overtook its assumptions.
URLs: https://www.airc.aist.go.jp/aitec-icot/ICOT/Museum/FinalReport/node2.html · https://handwiki.org/wiki/AI_winter

**14. Cyc — VERDICT: LENAT'S DEATH VERIFIED; START YEAR AND STATUS PARTLY UNVERIFIED.**
**Doug Lenat died 31 August 2023** (b. 13 September 1950) — VERIFIED. Standard account: Cyc began **1984** at **MCC** (Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation, Austin, Texas) and was spun out into **Cycorp** (1994), with Lenat as CEO. **COULD NOT VERIFY** the 1984/1994 dates against a primary Cycorp/MCC document this session. **Current status 2026: COULD NOT VERIFY authoritatively.** `cyc.com` was live at check time (so Cycorp still exists as a company), but widely circulated 2026 retrospective "Obituary for Cyc" pieces and their claims could not be read from this environment. Safe lecture wording: "Cyc has run for four decades — the longest-running symbolic-knowledge project — and never achieved its AGI ambitions; Lenat died in 2023."
URLs: https://www.legacy.com/news/douglas-lenat-1950-2023-pioneer-in-ai-development · https://cyc.com/leadership-team/

**15. 1980s expert-system boom and 1987 collapse; LISP-machine market — VERDICT: BROADLY CORRECT; SPECIFIC 1987 FIGURES UNVERIFIED.**
Boom: expert systems were adopted across corporations; by 1985 firms were spending over **$1 billion/year on AI**, most of it in-house; an industry grew up around them (Teknowledge, Intellicorp/KEE, and the hardware makers **Symbolics** and **LISP Machines Inc. (LMI)**). Collapse: the specialized LISP-hardware market eroded from **1987** onward — Sun and other UNIX workstations plus portable LISP (Lucid, Franz) removed the reason to buy a LISP machine; a contemporary 1987 source already describes the erosion (Stone, *AI Magazine* 8(1), Spring 1987, on Gold Hill's 80386 board: *"The 386 is the last nail in the coffin for Lisp machine leader Symbolics"*). **This was a gradual 1987–1993 decline, not a clean 1987/88 collapse** — the widely repeated "an industry worth half a billion dollars replaced in a single year" is a secondary (Wikipedia-derived) formulation, so state the trend rather than the figure. By the early 1990s most commercial LISP companies had failed (Symbolics, LMI, Lucid); TI and Xerox abandoned the field (Xerox spun off its AI unit on 24 August 1988). **Hard dated fact:** **Symbolics filed for Chapter 11 protection on 2 February 1993.** Detailed 1987 figures (Symbolics stock, layoffs, LMI's founding/end dates) **COULD NOT VERIFY** — do not put specific 1987 numbers on a slide without a fresh source.
URLs: https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1993-02-02-fi-1060-story.html · https://handwiki.org/wiki/AI_winter

## PARADIGM 2: BACKPROPAGATION AND CONNECTIONISM (1986–1995)

**16. Hopfield networks and the 2024 Nobel Prize — VERDICT: CORRECT, WITH PRIORITY NUANCE.**
John J. Hopfield, "Neural networks and physical systems with emergent collective computational abilities," *PNAS* **79**(8): 2554–2558, April 1982. DOI 10.1073/pnas.79.8.2554. Energy function **E = −Σ_{i<j} w_ij s_i s_j**, monotonically decreasing under asynchronous threshold dynamics; symmetric weights via the Hebb rule; stationary states are the stored memories. **There was no earlier 1982 Hopfield ANN paper** — his prior PNAS work was on electron transfer; the graded-response follow-up is *PNAS* **81**(10): 3088–3092, **May 1984**. **Priority:** the Nobel scientific background itself cites the precursor line, including **Amari 1972** (*IEEE Trans. Computers* C-21(11): 1197–1206) and **Little 1974** (*Mathematical Biosciences* 19(1–2): 101–120) — Hopfield did not invent associative memory; his contribution was the physics framing (energy function, collective computation, spin-glass tractability) that made it analysable and famous. **Nobel Physics 2024: announced 8 October 2024**, Hopfield and Hinton **1/2 each**, citation verbatim: *"for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks."*
URLs: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.79.8.2554 · https://api.nobelprize.org/2.1/nobelPrize/phy/2024 · https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/11/advanced-physicsprize2024-3.pdf

**17. Backpropagation attribution — VERDICT: MOSTLY CORRECT; THREE FIXES.**
- Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors," *Nature* **323**(6088): 533–536, October 1986. DOI 10.1038/323533a0. (Earlier UCSD tech report ICS-8506, 1985; and the PDP book chapter, MIT Press 1986.)
- **Linnainmaa (1970)** — master's thesis, University of Helsinki (published as "Taylor expansion of the accumulated rounding error," *BIT* **16**(2): 146–160, 1976) — invented **reverse-mode automatic differentiation**, *not* backprop-for-neural-nets. See Griewank, "Who Invented the Reverse Mode of Differentiation?", *Documenta Mathematica* Extra Volume ISMP (2012) 389–400.
- **Werbos 1974 is CONTESTED.** The Nobel scientific background cites his **1982** IFIP paper, not the 1974 thesis; Schmidhuber notes the 1974 thesis has only a preliminary, NN-specific discussion and that the first NN-specific efficient backprop "was apparently described by Werbos in 1982 (but not yet in his 1974 thesis, as is sometimes claimed)." Werbos disputes this. Do not assert "Werbos invented it in 1974" flatly.
- **FIX: "Anders Parker" → David B. Parker**, "Learning Logic," Technical Report TR-47, MIT Center for Computational Research in Economics and Management Science, April 1985. LeCun: "Une procédure d'apprentissage pour réseau à seuil asymétrique," *Cognitiva 85*, Paris, pp. 599–604; PhD thesis 1987.
- Control-theory precursors: Kelley, "Gradient Theory of Optimal Flight Paths," *ARS Journal* **30**(10): 947–954, Oct 1960; Bryson 1961; Bryson & Ho, *Applied Optimal Control*, 1969.
- **Honest attribution:** no single inventor. Kelley/Bryson/Dreyfus (1960–62) = continuous/control-theory precursor; Linnainmaa (1970) = efficient reverse-mode AD; Werbos (1982, building on his 1974 thesis) = first NN application; Parker (1985) and LeCun (1985) = independent rediscovery; Rumelhart–Hinton–Williams (1986) did not invent it but showed it learns useful hidden representations and popularised it.
URLs: https://doi.org/10.1038/323533a0 · https://people.idsia.ch/~juergen/who-invented-backpropagation.html · https://www.maths.tcd.ie/EMIS/journals/DMJDMV/vol-ismp/52_griewank-andreas-b.pdf

**18. Neocognitron (1980) and modern CNNs — VERDICT: CORRECT AS A PRECURSOR; TWO FIXES.**
Fukushima, "Neocognitron: A self-organizing neural network model for a mechanism of pattern recognition unaffected by shift in position," *Biological Cybernetics* **36**(4): 193–202, April 1980. DOI 10.1007/BF00344251. **Fix 1 — it did NOT use backprop:** zero occurrences of "back-propagation"; learning was unsupervised ("learning without a teacher", self-organisation by reinforcement of modifiable synapses). Supervised variants came in 1983 (Fukushima, Miyake & Ito) but still not BP. **Fix 2 — pooling was not max pooling:** C-cells pool over local S-cell groups through a saturating function φ[x] = x/(α+x) — saturating sum pooling. **Weight sharing: yes** — all cells in a cell-plane share the same synaptic distribution shifted in space, i.e. functionally convolution (though the word "convolution" never appears in the paper). Max pooling was formalised by Riesenhuber & Poggio, *Nature Neuroscience* 2(11): 1019–1025, 1999. **The lineage is official:** the 2024 Nobel scientific background states the CNN architecture "had its roots in the neocognitron method created by Kunihiko Fukushima," itself inspired by Hubel & Wiesel.
URLs: https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00344251 · https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr08/cos598B/Readings/Fukushima1980.pdf

**19. LeNet — VERDICT: CITATIONS AND DEPLOYMENT CORRECT; THE "BY 1996" DATE IS WRONG.**
- **1989 — VERIFIED.** LeCun, Boser, Denker, Henderson, Howard, Hubbard & Jackel (7 authors), "Backpropagation Applied to Handwritten Zip Code Recognition," *Neural Computation* **1**(4): 541–551, Winter/December 1989. DOI 10.1162/neco.1989.1.4.541. Task: isolated USPS zip-code digits from mail at the Buffalo, NY post office — 9,298 segmented numerals (7,291 train / 2,007 test); 5.0% raw test error, 12.1% rejections at 1% error. The 1989 network used a **16×16** normalized input.
- **1998 — VERIFIED.** LeCun, Bottou, Bengio & Haffner, "Gradient-Based Learning Applied to Document Recognition," *Proceedings of the IEEE* **86**(11): 2278–2324, November 1998. DOI 10.1109/5.726791 — the LeNet-5 paper. LeNet-5 input is **32×32** (7 layers + input). MNIST = "modified NIST", built from NIST Special Database 3 + 1, size-normalised to 20×20 and centred in 28×28; 60,000 train / 10,000 test. **What it "reads":** the check's **courtesy amount (the numeric amount field) only**, not full-page OCR — the paper says "For simplicity (and speed requirements), our initial task is to read the courtesy amount only." *Caution:* don't carry LeNet-5's 32×32 input back onto the 1989 network.
- **Deployment — REAL, BUT THE PERCENTAGE IS A ~2001/early-2000s FIGURE, NOT 1996.** The 1998 paper states the system "is in commercial use in the NCR Corporation line of check recognition systems for the banking industry… reading millions of checks per month in several banks across the United States," and that it "has been fielded in several banks across the United States **since June 1996**, and it has been reading millions of checks per day since then." **No percentage appears anywhere in the paper.** The 10–20% figure comes from Léon Bottou (co-author), ICML Workshop talk page for "Graph Transformer Networks" (2001): *"About 10% to 20% of the checks written in the U.S. since 1996 have been processed by a Graph Transformer Network"* — i.e. **cumulative since 1996, stated in 2001**, not a rate achieved by 1996. LeCun's own bio dates it later still: the system "was reading between 10 and 20% of all the checks in the US **in the early 2000s**."
- **PRECISE STATEMENT FOR THE SLIDE:** "This technology was integrated into NCR's check-reading line and fielded in several US banks from June 1996, reading millions of checks per day; by the early 2000s (a 2001 estimate by co-author Bottou) roughly 10–20% of US checks were being processed by the GTN-based system." **Do not write "by 1996."** Three ways the popular claim overshoots: (i) it is the courtesy-amount field that is read, not the whole check; (ii) it is a developers' estimate, not an audited market share; (iii) it is a range — "10%" and "10–20%" are both defensible, "20%" alone is not. The widely repeated "20 million checks/day = 10% of all US checks as of 2001" formulation is **untraceable to a citable primary source** — treat 20M/day as unverified. (A Federal Reserve release reporting 41.9B checks paid in 2000 is consistent with an early-2000s figure and inconsistent with 10–20% being reached during 1996.)
URLs: https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1989.1.4.541 · https://doi.org/10.1109/5.726791 · https://leon.bottou.org/talks/gtn · http://lecun.com/ex/bio.html · https://hal.science/hal-03926082/document

**20. Vanishing gradient — VERDICT: SUBSTANTIALLY CORRECT; CREDIT BELONGS PRIMARILY TO HOCHREITER (1991).**
Hochreiter's **1991** TU Munich diploma thesis, *"Untersuchungen zu dynamischen neuronalen Netzen"* (15 June 1991; supervisor Jürgen Schmidhuber), contains the first detailed mathematical analysis of exponentially **vanishing/exploding error backflow** (§2 "Untersuchungen zum Fehlerrückfluß"; §4 "Konstanter Fehlerrückfluß", the CEC precursor). **Bengio, Simard & Frasconi**, "Learning long-term dependencies with gradient descent is difficult," *IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks* **5**(2): 157–166, March 1994, DOI 10.1109/72.279181, is the independent treatment that became the widely cited one. **Mozer (1989)** documented the symptom (with adaptive time constants) but not the mechanism. The 1997 LSTM paper recaps Hochreiter's 1991 analysis explicitly: error signals "tend to either (1) blow up or (2) vanish."
URLs: https://www.bioinf.jku.at/publications/older/3804.pdf · https://doi.org/10.1109/72.279181

**21. LSTM — VERDICT: CORRECT.**
Sepp Hochreiter & Jürgen Schmidhuber, **"Long Short-Term Memory," *Neural Computation* 9(8): 1735–1780, 15 November 1997**. DOI 10.1162/neco.1997.9.8.1735. **The forget gate was NOT in the 1997 paper** — the text contains zero occurrences of "forget"; it introduces only a multiplicative input gate, a multiplicative output gate, and the constant error carousel. The forget gate was added by **Gers, Schmidhuber & Cummins**: conference version "Learning to forget: continual prediction with LSTM," *ICANN '99*, IEE Conf. Pub. 470, pp. 850–855, DOI 10.1049/cp:19991218; journal version **"Learning to Forget: Continual Prediction with LSTM," *Neural Computation* 12(10): 2451–2471, October 2000**, DOI 10.1162/089976600300015015. (Peephole connections: Gers & Schmidhuber, IJCNN 2000.)
URLs: https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1997.9.8.1735 · https://direct.mit.edu/neco/article-abstract/12/10/2451/6415/Learning-to-Forget-Continual-Prediction-with-LSTM

**22. The "second AI winter" — VERDICT: CONTESTED. Right in outline; wrong in several specifics and in its framing.**
**What is solid.** (a) **Expert systems:** the contraction is real but only **qualitatively** documented — Nilsson gives the mechanism: because clients could build their own systems on cheap workstations and PCs, many expert-system companies *"ceased to exist, were bought by larger companies, or had to reorient their businesses"* (ch. 18.2.4). No market-size, revenue-drop, layoff or stock-collapse figure could be verified — **do not put such numbers on a slide.** (b) **LISP machines: a gradual 1987–1993 decline, not a clean 1987/88 collapse** — a contemporary 1987 source already describes erosion (Stone, *AI Magazine* 8(1), Spring 1987, on Gold Hill's 80386 board: *"The 386 is the last nail in the coffin for Lisp machine leader Symbolics"*). Terminal event: **Symbolics Chapter 11, 2 February 1993**; Xerox spun off its AI unit on 24 August 1988. (c) **DARPA — programmatic, not a "1987 review":** SCI was written up in Oct 1983 ($50M in FY1984; ~$1bn total 1983–1993); **Jacob Schwartz became ISTO director in September 1987** and cancelled some AI programs and failed to renew others; the Autonomous Land Vehicle was cancelled in April 1988; SCI "vanished from the DARPA budget in 1993." Hard number: **DARPA's basic-AI + SCI research line fell from $47M to $31M between 1987 and 1989** (that line, not the whole SCI budget). **The popular "1987 DARPA review" and "three-year funding cuts" causal story COULD NOT VERIFY — do not use it.**
**Where the claim needs fixing most — the historiography.** **Nothing supports "as severe as the first winter."** **Nilsson never uses the phrase "second AI winter":** his section is titled **24.4 The "AI Winter"** — with scare quotes — and he dates it to **"the mid- to late 1980s," not the early 1990s**; his verdict is that *"the winter endured only for a season – a season not of hibernation but of renewed efforts to carry on"* (AAAI membership levelled at 4,000–5,000 by 1996). Note also that **the term predates the downturn** — at the 1984 AAAI convention a panel titled *"The 'Dark Ages' of AI – Can We Avoid Them or Survive Them?"* was chaired by **Drew McDermott**, who said *"I think it is important that we take steps to make sure the 'AI Winter' doesn't happen"* — so the narrative was partly constructed **before** the contraction. Hirsch-Kreinsen (*AI & Society* 39:1641–1652, 2024) explicitly rejects the all-crisis reading: *"It would be wrong to view this solely as a crisis phase; rather it was a long phase of AI consolidation,"* pointing to the shift to Bayesian/statistical methods and the neural-network revival. "Second AI winter" **is** used by some scholarly sources but with loose dating — e.g. Klarmann, arXiv:2103.11961: *"The second AI winter (around 1987)."*
**Suggested rewrite for the slide:** "The late-1980s/early-1990s downturn was a real commercial contraction of symbolic AI: expert-systems vendors lost their market to cheap workstations and in-house development, Symbolics filed Chapter 11 in February 1993, DARPA's basic-AI plus Strategic Computing research line fell from $47M to $31M between 1987 and 1989, and Japan's FGCS closed in FY1992 (¥54bn ≈ $380M) with a two-year follow-on. But standard histories do not call it a uniformly 'second AI winter': Nilsson dates it to the mid-to-late 1980s, never uses the phrase, and says it 'endured only for a season'; Hirsch-Kreinsen says viewing the 1990s 'solely as a crisis phase' 'would be wrong.' The 1987–1993 label and the dollar figures for the expert-systems market are popular framings, not sourced periodization."

## GENERAL

**41. "Two winters" vs "multiple boom–bust cycles" — VERDICT: CONTESTED; REFRAME.**
Don't say "AI has had two winters" as flat fact. Say: **AI has gone through repeated boom-and-bust episodes, two of them major** — c.1974–1980 and c.1987–2000 — **plus several smaller episodes**: 1966 (machine translation / ALPAC), 1969 (*Perceptrons*), 1971–75 (DARPA Speech Understanding Research), 1973 (Lighthill, UK), 1973–74 (DARPA academic cutbacks), 1987 (LISP-machine market collapse), 1988 (Strategic Computing Initiative spending cancelled), 1990s (expert systems abandoned; FGCS goals unmet). The reference account itself warns "Different sources use different dates for the AI winter." **The framing is genuinely contested:** **Thomas Haigh, "There Was No 'First AI Winter'," *Communications of the ACM* 66(12), December 2023, 35–39, DOI 10.1145/3625833**, argues that AI activity and professional interest did not slow in the 1970s — using ACM SIGART membership as a proxy — even as US defence funding was redirected. (Haigh has a five-part CACM series on AI history, 66:6 (2023) through 68:2 (2025), plus a forthcoming MIT Press book, working title *Artificial Intelligence: The History of a Brand*.) *Caveat:* the specific SIGART figures in the handwiki summary (1,241 members in 1973 → ~3,500 by mid-1978) **COULD NOT VERIFY independently** — ACM pages are Cloudflare-blocked from this environment; treat them as Haigh's data pending a direct read. **Term coinage — PARTLY CORRECT, IMPRECISE:** the "AI winter" metaphor was popularised in the **mid-1980s**, commonly traced to **Roger Schank's** warning at the **1984 AAAI** meeting, published as "The Dark Ages of AI: A Panel Discussion at AAAI-84," *AI Magazine* **6**(3), Fall 1985, p. 122, DOI 10.1609/aimag.v6i3.494. Two fixes: the panel's own title is **"The Dark Ages of AI," not "AI winter"**; and **Minsky is not among the five published panelists** — so the common "Schank *and Minsky* coined 'AI winter' at AAAI-84" version is unsupported by the published record. **The 1984 panel was chaired by Drew McDermott**, and per Nilsson's account (ch. 24.4) its title was *"The 'Dark Ages' of AI – Can We Avoid Them or Survive Them?"* with McDermott saying *"I think it is important that we take steps to make sure the 'AI Winter' doesn't happen"* — which is a **1984 occurrence of the exact phrase** and confirms that **the "winter" narrative was partly constructed before the downturn**, not after it.

## COMMON MYTHS TO AVOID

These are the claims in the list that are **wrong or misleading**, ranked by how likely they are to embarrass a speaker on stage. (13 rather than the requested 5–10, because each is a genuine error; the top 10 are the highest-risk.)

1. **"The term 'artificial intelligence' was coined at the 1956 Dartmouth workshop."** It appears in the **31 August 1955 proposal** ("…a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence…"). The workshop popularised it; the proposal named it.
2. **"Minsky & Papert's *Perceptrons* killed neural networks / proved multilayer nets can't do XOR."** It proved **single-layer** limits (XOR/parity, connectedness). The authors already knew MLPs *could* compute XOR; the missing piece was a **hidden-layer training algorithm**. And Rosenblatt died in **1971**, not on publication.
3. **"In 1965 Simon said a computer would be world chess champion within 10 years; and *Life* 1970 promised a robot in 20 years."** Three separate errors: chess-in-10 is **Simon & Newell 1957/58** (*Operations Research* 6(1):1–15); the "within twenty years… any work a man can do" line is **Simon 1960** (reprinted 1965, hence the misattribution); and the *Life* 1970 line is **Minsky's "three to eight years,"** not 20.
4. **"MYCIN was evaluated in the 1980s."** The famous blinded evaluation is **JAMA, 21 September 1979** — and MYCIN was **never used on patients**.
5. **"XCON saved DEC $40M per year."** **Not traceable to a primary document.** The 1984 AI Magazine account has no dollar figure; documented numbers are **~$25M/year** and Newquist's **$40M over six years**. Rules reached **3,303 by November 1983**, not ~2,500.
6. **"The Mark I Perceptron was 1957/1958 hardware."** 1957 = the **IBM 704 simulation**; the Mark I was assembled in **1959** and first demonstrated **23 June 1960**.
7. **"GPT-1 is arXiv 1806.08775."** That ID is **"CVC4 at the SMT Competition 2018."** GPT-1 has **no arXiv version** (OpenAI tech report, June 2018) and its paper **states no parameter count**, so "117M" should be hedged.
8. **"Chinchilla corrected Kaplan's learning-rate-schedule bug."** Not the established cause. The peer-reviewed reconciliation (Pearce & Song, TMLR 2024) attributes the gap to **non-embedding vs total parameter counting plus small-scale analysis**. Use the **20-tokens-per-parameter** headline finding instead.
9. **"Six of the eight Transformer authors left Google; Vaswani and Jones stayed."** **All eight have left**, and **Parmar went to Anthropic, not OpenAI**; Shazeer **returned** to Google in Aug 2024 before leaving again for OpenAI in 2026.
10. **"AlexNet's 15.3% and ResNet's 3.57% are single-model results."** Both are **ensembles** — AlexNet's 15.3% averages 7 CNNs (single net: 18.2% top-5); ResNet's 3.57% averages 6 models (single 152-layer: 4.49% val top-5).
11. **"AI has had two winters."** Contested framing — say "repeated boom–bust episodes, two of them major," and cite **Haigh, CACM, December 2023** for the first "winter" being largely retrospective. For the second: **Nilsson never uses the phrase "second AI winter,"** dates his section 24.4 The "AI Winter" to **the mid-to-late 1980s**, and says it "endured only for a season"; **Hirsch-Kreinsen (2024)** says viewing the 1990s "solely as a crisis phase" "would be wrong." Also: the **LISP-machine decline was gradual (1987–1993)**, not a clean 1987 collapse, and **nothing supports "as severe as the first winter."**
12. **"Deep Blue's 1996 match was 10–14 February."** It ran **10–17 February 1996**.
13. **"By 1996 NCR was reading 10–20% of all US checks."** The deployment is real — fielded in US banks **from June 1996**, millions of checks per day — but **no percentage appears in the 1998 paper**. The 10–20% figure is co-author **Bottou's 2001** estimate of checks processed **cumulatively since 1996**, and LeCun's bio places it in **the early 2000s**. Also, only the **courtesy-amount field** is read, not the whole check.
